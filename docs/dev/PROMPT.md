# SSEVOH-DSL: Development, Formal Assurance, and Empirical Evaluation Plan

**Full title:** A Domain-Specific Language and Formal Verification Framework for Safe Self-Evolution of AI Agent Harnesses

**Central philosophy:**

> Agents may propose their own evolution, but every evolution must earn permission through explicit constraints, verification, and evidence.

## 1. Project Mission

Develop SSEVOH-DSL, a Domain-Specific Language and supporting formal verification framework for specifying, analysing, controlling, and validating the self-evolution of AI agent harnesses.

The fundamental problem is that modern AI agents increasingly modify their own operational environments. These modifications may involve skills, memories, tools, execution policies, workflows, configurations, and other reusable components.

Although autonomous evolution can improve agent capabilities, it introduces significant engineering risks:

- An agent may introduce modifications beyond its intended permissions.
- A locally beneficial modification may break dependent components.
- A previously validated component may become unsafe after another component changes.
- Derived artifacts may retain dependencies on withdrawn artifacts.
- Rollback may unintentionally restore previously prohibited components.
- Changes may be difficult to explain, reproduce, or audit.
- An agent may gradually expand its own privileges or weaken its safety restrictions.

Traditional version control, logging, and configuration validation can help manage these problems, but they do not independently establish that an evolution is permitted or safe.

SSEVOH-DSL addresses this challenge by introducing an explicit, machine-readable language for describing evolution requirements and a verification pipeline that independently evaluates proposed modifications.

### Primary objective

Build a framework in which an AI agent can propose modifications to its harness, but cannot directly deploy them without satisfying the applicable constraints, verification requirements, and evidence requirements.

The framework must separate three responsibilities:

1. **Evolution proposal:** The AI agent determines what it wants to change.
2. **Evolution assurance:** SSEVOH-DSL independently determines whether the proposed change is structurally valid, authorized, and sufficiently verified.
3. **Evolution enforcement:** A controlled execution mechanism applies only approved modifications.

The safety of the framework must not depend on an LLM correctly following its instructions.

## 2. Research Questions

Use these five research questions to guide the project.

**RQ1 — Expressiveness:** To what extent can SSEVOH-DSL express the important aspects of AI agent harness evolution, including modification permissions, dependencies, provenance, validation, deployment, withdrawal, and rollback?

**RQ2 — Formal assurance:** To what extent can static analysis and formal verification detect unsafe, inconsistent, or unauthorized evolution scenarios before deployment?

**RQ3 — Enforcement reliability:** How effectively does the framework prevent unsafe evolution proposals from being deployed, including proposals generated by different LLMs?

**RQ4 — Efficiency:** What computational and operational overhead does SSEVOH-DSL introduce compared with less restrictive evolution approaches?

**RQ5 — Scalability:** How does the framework scale with increasing numbers of harness components, dependencies, evolution operations, and concurrent modifications?

These questions should guide the implementation and experiments. The IEEE Software article should emphasize the practical findings, while the supplementary documents should provide the complete technical evidence.

## 3. Core Design Principles

The following principles must govern all architectural and implementation decisions.

### 3.1 Proposal is not permission

An AI agent proposing a modification does not authorize that modification.

### 3.2 Verification before deployment

Required validation and verification must complete before an evolution can enter the trusted deployed state.

### 3.3 Explicit evolution boundaries

The language must describe what can change, what cannot change, and under which conditions modifications are permitted.

### 3.4 Evidence-based approval

Approval must be supported by the required evidence rather than an LLM's assertion that a change is safe.

### 3.5 Independent enforcement

The agent proposing an evolution must not be able to bypass or modify the authorization and verification mechanisms protecting its own deployment.

### 3.6 Evolution-aware provenance

The framework must track not only what changed but also why it changed, where the change originated, which artifacts depend on it, and what validation supports it.

### 3.7 Safe withdrawal and recovery

Withdrawal and rollback must be treated as explicit lifecycle operations with their own constraints and verification requirements.

### 3.8 Verifiable semantics

The runtime implementation and formal specification must describe the same evolution lifecycle.

## 4. Conceptual Architecture

Develop the framework around the following components.

### 4.1 SSEVOH-DSL

Design a declarative language for describing:

- Harness components and their types.
- Component versions and trusted states.
- Evolution operations.
- Component dependencies.
- Permissions and restrictions.
- Preconditions and postconditions.
- Safety invariants.
- Validation requirements.
- Evidence requirements.
- Provenance relationships.
- Deployment and withdrawal conditions.
- Rollback and recovery requirements.

The DSL must be understandable to practicing software engineers rather than designed exclusively for formal-methods specialists.

### 4.2 DSL compiler

Implement a complete language-processing pipeline:

1. Lexical analysis.
2. Parsing.
3. Abstract syntax tree construction.
4. Semantic analysis.
5. Type checking.
6. Dependency resolution.
7. Intermediate representation generation.
8. Static policy validation.
9. Formal verification translation.

Diagnostics must distinguish syntax errors, semantic errors, type errors, policy violations, and verification failures.

### 4.3 Evolution assurance engine

This is the central component of SSEVOH-DSL.

It must evaluate every proposed evolution against the applicable constraints and determine whether sufficient evidence exists to authorize deployment.

The engine should support:

- Proposal validation.
- Permission checking.
- Preconditions and postconditions.
- Dependency impact analysis.
- Provenance validation.
- Safety invariant checking.
- Formal verification.
- Evidence evaluation.
- Approval or rejection.
- Revalidation when necessary.

The engine must be deterministic wherever possible.

### 4.4 Evolution execution engine

Implement a controlled mechanism for applying approved changes.

It must support:

- Creating an isolated candidate state.
- Applying approved modifications.
- Recording the resulting state.
- Maintaining provenance.
- Handling interrupted operations.
- Supporting withdrawal and recovery.
- Preventing partial or unauthorized updates.

Keep deployment adapters separate from verification logic.

The initial implementation should use a controlled simulation or sandbox adapter rather than modifying production AI agents.

### 4.5 Formal verification engine

Translate SSEVOH-DSL specifications into an appropriate formal representation.

Investigate:

- TLA+ and TLC for state-transition systems and temporal properties.
- Alloy for structural relationships and constraints.
- SMT solving for constraint checking and satisfiability.

Select the smallest combination that provides adequate assurance.

Do not introduce multiple formal tools merely to increase technical complexity.

## 5. DSL Design and Semantic Scope

The DSL should be small, expressive, and extensible.

### 5.1 Core language concepts

Distinguish the following entities:

- **Harness:** The complete operational environment of an AI agent.
- **Component:** An independently identifiable harness artifact.
- **Version:** A specific state of a component.
- **Dependency:** A relationship between components.
- **Evolution:** A proposed transformation of the harness.
- **Policy:** A rule defining permitted or prohibited evolution.
- **Evidence:** Information supporting a verification or approval decision.
- **Validation:** An activity establishing whether specified requirements are satisfied.
- **Deployment:** Application of an approved evolution to the trusted harness.

### 5.2 Evolution lifecycle

Define an explicit lifecycle, initially considering:

`PROPOSED → ANALYSED → VERIFIED → APPROVED → DEPLOYED`

Include rejection, withdrawal, revalidation, and recovery states.

Do not assume that the lifecycle is always linear.

For example, a change to a dependency may invalidate an earlier approval and require revalidation.

The final lifecycle must be formally defined as a transition system.

### 5.3 Evolution permissions

Investigate language constructs for specifying:

- Immutable components.
- Modifiable components.
- Authorized modification types.
- Restricted operations.
- Required approval levels.
- Protected dependencies.
- Privilege boundaries.
- Conditions for granting temporary permissions.

An agent must never be allowed to grant itself additional privileges simply by modifying its own specification.

### 5.4 Provenance and dependency closure

Represent relationships between:

- Original artifacts.
- Derived artifacts.
- Transformations.
- Validation evidence.
- Deployments.
- Subsequent modifications.

Investigate dependency closure as a basis for determining which artifacts are affected by a modification or withdrawal.

A critical distinction must be preserved: withdrawing an original artifact does not necessarily mean every artifact derived from it can simply be deleted.

Some derived artifacts may have additional dependencies or independent contributions.

Define precise rules for identifying affected artifacts and determining whether they must be withdrawn, revalidated, or retained.

### 5.5 Rollback semantics

Rollback must not be implemented as an unconditional restoration of an old snapshot.

Consider the following scenario:

1. Component A is trusted.
2. Component B is derived from A.
3. A is subsequently withdrawn.
4. A later evolution fails and requests rollback to a snapshot containing A.

A naive rollback would reintroduce a withdrawn artifact.

SSEVOH-DSL must explicitly address this conflict.

Define when exact rollback is permitted, when selective recovery is necessary, and when recovery must be rejected.

### 5.6 Evidence and approval

Distinguish between:

- Evidence that a modification was proposed.
- Evidence that the modification is authorized.
- Evidence that structural constraints are satisfied.
- Evidence that required properties were verified.
- Evidence that deployment completed successfully.

An LLM-generated explanation must not automatically count as independent verification evidence.

## 6. Formal Properties and Verification Strategy

Create a dedicated formal specification before implementing the verification engine.

At minimum, investigate the following properties.

| Property | Intended guarantee |
|---|---|
| Authorization safety | Unauthorized evolution cannot be deployed. |
| State integrity | Deployed states satisfy required structural constraints. |
| Dependency integrity | Required dependencies remain valid. |
| Provenance integrity | Deployed modifications have traceable origins. |
| Evidence completeness | Required evidence exists before approval. |
| Withdrawal safety | Withdrawal cannot leave prohibited dependent artifacts active. |
| Rollback safety | Recovery cannot silently restore withdrawn artifacts. |
| Revalidation safety | Relevant changes invalidate affected previous approvals. |
| Isolation | Evolution cannot silently modify unrelated protected components. |
| Privilege integrity | Agents cannot bypass their assigned evolution permissions. |
| Lifecycle correctness | Only permitted lifecycle transitions can occur. |

For every property:

1. Define its informal meaning.
2. Define its mathematical representation.
3. Identify the relevant state variables and transitions.
4. Specify assumptions.
5. Develop positive and negative scenarios.
6. Implement the corresponding verification model.
7. Record verification outcomes and counterexamples.

Distinguish safety from liveness.

Safety means that something bad never happens. Liveness means that something good can eventually happen.

For example, preventing unauthorized deployment is a safety property. Ensuring that an eligible evolution can eventually be deployed is a liveness concern.

### Formal assurance deliverables

Create independently compilable LaTeX documents containing:

- Mathematical system definitions.
- Harness-state definitions.
- Operational semantics.
- Transition-system definitions.
- Formal safety properties.
- Proof arguments.
- Model-checking configurations.
- Verification results.
- Counterexamples.
- Assumptions and limitations.

Do not claim universal safety for arbitrary real-world implementations based solely on model checking an abstract system.

### Phase 2 — Evolution lifecycle and assurance engine

Implement:

- Evolution state machine.
- Harness reference model.
- Permission checking.
- Preconditions and postconditions.
- Dependency tracking.
- Provenance.
- Evidence validation.
- Approval and rejection.

- Missing evidence prevents approval.

### Phase 3 — Formal verification

Implement the translation from DSL specifications to the selected formal models.

Start with small models and progressively increase complexity.

Run:

- Structural constraint checks.
- Safety-property verification.
- Temporal-property verification where applicable.
- Negative scenarios.
- State-space exploration.
- Counterexample analysis.
- Regression verification.

Acceptance criteria:

- Every claimed property maps to an executable verification artifact or documented proof argument.
- Intentionally unsafe models produce counterexamples.
- Corrected models pass their corresponding checks.
- Results are reproducible from versioned specifications and configurations.

### Phase 4 — Comprehensive testing

Develop:

**Unit tests:** Parser, type checker, IR, policy evaluation, dependency resolution, provenance, lifecycle transitions, and recovery.

**Integration tests:** Complete compilation-to-verification-to-deployment workflows.

**Property-based tests:** Generate random harnesses and evolution sequences and check invariants after every permitted transition.

**Metamorphic tests:** Apply transformations expected to preserve specified properties and check consistency.

**Negative tests:** Attempt unauthorized changes, privilege escalation, invalid dependencies, missing evidence, and prohibited restoration.

**Mutation tests:** Deliberately weaken safety checks and establish whether the test suite detects the resulting faults.

**Concurrency tests:** Test simultaneous modifications, conflicts, and race conditions.

**Fault-injection tests:** Simulate interrupted deployments, missing artifacts, failed validation, and recovery failures.

**Regression tests:** Preserve every discovered defect as a permanent test.

Maintain a requirements-to-property-to-test traceability matrix.

Do not confuse test success with formal proof.

### Phase 5 — HeartBench development

Develop the benchmark described in Section 9.

Ensure that the benchmark has independently established ground truth and supports both deterministic and LLM-assisted evaluation.

### Phase 6 — HPC qualification and experiments

Use `use-hpc` to qualify Fir, Narval, and Rorqual, select the most effective execution strategy, and run the full five-model experiment.

### Phase 7 — Analysis and publication

Complete statistical analysis, generate figures and tables, compile the supplementary documents, and write the IEEE Software article.

Do not write empirical conclusions before the corresponding results exist.

## 9. Empirical Evaluation: HeartBench

Develop a benchmark called **HeartBench**.

Its purpose is to assess the expressiveness, enforcement effectiveness, verification performance, and practical cost of SSEVOH-DSL.

Use two complementary evaluation layers.

### 9.1 Deterministic evolution benchmark

Create scenarios with known expected outcomes.

Include:

1. Safe skill modification.
2. Unauthorized tool permission expansion.
3. Memory modification with dependent artifacts.
4. Invalid workflow dependencies.
5. Protected configuration modification.
6. Deployment without required evidence.
7. Withdrawal of an original artifact with derived descendants.
8. Rollback after subsequent withdrawal.
9. Revalidation following dependency changes.
10. Concurrent evolution requests.
11. Partial deployment failure.
12. Privilege escalation attempts.
13. Cross-agent dependency conflicts.
14. Provenance corruption.
15. Recovery following interrupted evolution.

Each scenario must include:

- Initial harness state.
- Proposed modification.
- Applicable policies.
- Expected authorization decision.
- Expected verification outcome.
- Expected state after execution.
- Relevant safety properties.
- Expected provenance records.
- Expected rejection reason or counterexample.

Include both valid and invalid scenarios, including difficult boundary cases.

Ground truth must be specified independently of the implementation being evaluated.

### 9.2 LLM-assisted evolution benchmark

Evaluate five Ollama models.

For each task, provide:

- Natural-language change request.
- Harness description.
- Relevant evolution policies.
- Required output format.

Ask the model to produce a candidate SSEVOH-DSL evolution specification.

The verification engine must independently evaluate the generated proposal.

Measure:

- Syntax validity.
- Semantic validity.
- Permission compliance.
- Dependency correctness.
- Evidence completeness.
- Withdrawal and rollback correctness.
- Safe proposal acceptance.
- Unsafe proposal rejection.

The most important distinction is between the model's ability to generate a safe proposal and the framework's ability to prevent an unsafe proposal from being deployed.

A dangerous proposal correctly rejected by SSEVOH-DSL is evidence of successful enforcement, not an unsafe deployment.

An unsafe proposal incorrectly accepted is a critical failure.

## 12. Evaluation Metrics

Collect metrics at four levels.

### DSL quality

- Valid specification rate.
- Semantic validation success.
- Type-error detection.
- Diagnostic quality.
- Expressiveness across benchmark scenarios.
- Specification complexity.

### Verification effectiveness

- Unsafe scenarios correctly rejected.
- Safe scenarios correctly accepted.
- False acceptance rate.
- False rejection rate.
- Safety-property coverage.
- Counterexample generation success.
- Verification completion rate.
- State-space size.
- Verification execution time.

The false acceptance rate is especially important: it measures the proportion of ground-truth unsafe proposals accepted by the complete enforcement pipeline.

### Operational performance

- Compilation time.
- Static analysis time.
- Verification time.
- Deployment simulation time.
- Total validation latency.
- CPU and memory consumption.
- GPU memory and utilization where relevant.
- Provenance-recording overhead.

### LLM performance

For each model:

- Syntax validity.
- Semantic validity.
- Task completion rate.
- Permission compliance.
- Unsafe proposal generation rate.
- Verification rejection rate.
- Accepted-safe proposal rate.
- Token consumption.
- Generation latency.
- Throughput.
- Retry frequency.

Distinguish:

1. Valid and safe proposals.
2. Unsafe proposals correctly rejected.
3. Unsafe proposals incorrectly accepted.

The third category represents the most serious failure.

### Statistical analysis

Report:

- Sample sizes.
- Mean and median.
- Standard deviation.
- 95% confidence intervals where appropriate.
- Effect sizes.
- Suitable statistical comparisons.

Use paired comparisons for models receiving identical tasks.

Account for task difficulty and repeated measurements.

Do not report statistical significance without an appropriate test and adequate observations.

## 13. Baselines and Ablation Studies

Implement the following configurations:

**B0 — Unrestricted baseline:** Apply proposals without SSEVOH-DSL verification in a sandbox.

**B1 — Basic validation:** Use structural and schema validation without full evolution policies.

**B2 — Static SSEVOH-DSL:** Use compilation, type checking, and static policy validation without formal model checking.

**B3 — Full SSEVOH-DSL:** Use the complete language, semantic analysis, dependency analysis, and applicable formal verification.

**B4 — LLM-assisted SSEVOH-DSL:** Use the full framework with the five Ollama models.

Conduct additional ablations by disabling:

- Dependency closure analysis.
- Provenance checks.
- Withdrawal validation.
- Rollback conflict checks.
- Revalidation rules.

Measure which failures each removed mechanism allows through.

All weakened configurations must remain isolated in the sandbox.

## 14. Threats to Validity

Explicitly document:

- Benchmark representativeness.
- Synthetic scenario bias.
- Model and quantization differences.
- Hardware and scheduling variability.
- LLM stochasticity.
- Formal abstraction limitations.
- Translation correctness.
- Differences between simulated and production deployment.
- Limited model selection.

Mitigate these threats through independent ground truth, repeated trials, cross-cluster measurements, regression tests, and explicit assumptions.

## 22. Final Research Vision

SSEVOH-DSL should establish a practical engineering principle for self-evolving AI systems:

**Autonomous agents should have the freedom to propose improvements, but not the authority to unilaterally redefine the boundaries within which they operate.**

The DSL makes these boundaries explicit. The formal verification engine checks whether proposed evolution satisfies them. The evidence and authorization mechanisms establish whether deployment is justified. The controlled execution engine ensures that only approved changes are applied.

The final outcome must be a working, reproducible, and rigorously evaluated framework that makes self-evolution of AI agent harnesses explicit, constrained, traceable, and formally analysable.

The research contribution is not simply another configuration language for AI agents. It is an engineering foundation for making autonomous evolution accountable and verifiable before changes become part of a trusted system.
