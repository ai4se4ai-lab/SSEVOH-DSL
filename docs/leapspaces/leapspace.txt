### Direct Answer

The literature strongly supports the research motivation behind **SSEVOH-DSL**: self-evolving agents do benefit from modifying prompts, tools, workflows, and related control artifacts, but the evidence also shows these changes are unstable, failure-prone, and poorly served by one-time testing alone [9] [10] [25] [26] [27]. Across governance-oriented work, the most defensible design pattern is to represent agent changes explicitly, validate them before use, and mediate activation through trusted infrastructure such as policy checks, approval gates, signed queues, canary release, rollback, and audit logging [16] [18] [19] [20] [21] [43] [44] [45].

Taken together, these findings suggest that a **DSL-plus-trusted-approval-service** approach is both well motivated and meaningfully differentiated from informal prompt editing, because the surveyed literature contains many partial ingredients of governance but comparatively few end-to-end systems that formally describe self-modifications and require trusted pre-activation approval for every change [12] [13] [46] [47]. The clearest research opportunities for SSEVOH-DSL are: typed change representations, repeated re-validation, approval-aware activation semantics, rollback and revocation, and better governance of memory updates, which remain relatively weakly studied [5] [7] [8] [36].

**Confidence level: High** - multiple independent findings converge on the same governance need across formal methods, trusted execution, and empirical agent-evaluation literature [1] [5] [16] [40].

**Confidence level: High** - the supplied evidence spans both conceptual and empirical strands directly relevant to governed self-evolution [1] [38].

### Assumptions & Limitations

- The supplied evidence does **not** establish that any existing framework exactly matches SSEVOH-DSL’s full combination of prompt, tool, memory, and workflow self-modification with mandatory trusted approval before every activation; comparisons are therefore adjacent rather than exact [13] [20].  
- Evidence is strongest for workflow, policy, and tool governance, and weakest for memory-update governance, memory drift detection, and rollback semantics [36].  
- Some governance sources come from adjacent autonomous-system domains rather than direct LLM-agent self-modification, so transfer to SSEVOH-DSL is a reasoned synthesis rather than a direct one-to-one equivalence [16] [20] [46].  

**Confidence level: High** - these limits are explicitly stated in the evidence synthesis [5] [36].

### Suggested Further Research

- Build end-to-end systems that treat each self-modification as a typed, approvable change request with preconditions, invariants, rollback plans, and post-deployment monitors [5] [7] [8].  
- Develop benchmarks for governed memory evolution, including stale-memory insertion, contradictory updates, unauthorized edits, and delayed harm from previously approved memories [36].  
- Compare DSL-mediated approval against informal prompt editing and lightweight policy checking in controlled experiments [12] [47].  
- Evaluate repeated re-validation over time, not just one-shot testing, especially under changing tools, environments, and tasks [6] [7] [10].

## Full Report

## 1. Introduction: Self-Evolving Agents and the Governance Problem

Self-evolving agents are increasingly designed to improve themselves by rewriting or restructuring prompts, policies, tools, memory, and workflows rather than remaining fixed after deployment [1] [2] [3] [49]. This trend is attractive because adaptive revision can improve task performance without retraining and can exploit failures as signals for repair, reflection, or re-planning [23] [28] [50] [51] [52]. However, the same adaptivity creates a governance problem: a change that passed one validation step may later become unsafe, invalid, or ineffective as tasks, environments, tools, or internal state evolve [5] [7] [8] [9] [10].

The central contribution claimed for SSEVOH-DSL—formal description of agent self-modifications plus mandatory trusted approval before activation—aligns closely with this problem formulation [1] [43] [45]. The literature surveyed here suggests three reasons this framing is timely. First, structured representations improve auditability and correctness relative to informal prompt editing [1] [2] [12]. Second, runtime approval and trusted mediation are increasingly used in governance-first architectures to control when updates take effect [16] [18] [19] [20]. Third, empirical agent studies show that self-improvement is real but unstable, making repeated validation more defensible than indefinite trust [25] [26] [27].

**Confidence level: High** - the motivation is directly supported across both governance and empirical literatures [5] [25].

## 2. Foundational Context

### 2.1 What counts as self-modification?

Within the supplied evidence, self-modification spans at least four surfaces:

- **Prompt-level change**: revising instructions, reflective prompts, or behavior-guiding text [23] [24].  
- **Tool-level change**: selecting tools, changing tool-use plans, invoking APIs differently, or recovering from tool faults [31] [32] [34].  
- **Memory-level change**: updating persistent knowledge or retrieved context used by the agent, though this area remains weakly benchmarked [36].  
- **Workflow-level change**: altering plans, SOPs, orchestration logic, policies, or control flows through structured specifications [1] [11] [14] [15].

This matters because different modification surfaces imply different validation burdens: prompt changes may alter reasoning style, tool changes may trigger unsafe actions, memory changes may create delayed errors, and workflow changes may affect long-horizon behavior [29] [35].

### 2.2 Why representation and activation must be separated

A recurring theme is that representing a change and activating it safely are distinct problems [12] [13]. Formal or semi-formal languages help specify what a change is, but trust depends on how that change is checked, approved, executed, monitored, and revoked at runtime [7] [43] [44] [45]. This separation is especially important when agents adapt after deployment, because a valid description alone does not guarantee continued safety or utility in new contexts [5] [17].

### 2.3 Why outcome-only validation is insufficient

The evidence rejects the idea that final task success alone is enough to validate evolving agents [40] [42]. Two systems may succeed on the same benchmark while following very different trajectories, using tools differently, or incurring different safety risks [40]. For self-modifying agents, this implies that approval decisions should consider not only whether the modified agent can succeed, but how it behaves during execution and whether the modification remains trustworthy over time [38] [41].

**Confidence level: High** - these concepts recur consistently across the supplied sources [5] [40].

## 3. Formal Languages, DSLs, and Executable Specifications for Agent Change

### 3.1 Structured specification as a governance mechanism

Several strands of work argue that externalizing behavior into structured specifications improves correctness, transparency, and human oversight. DSLs can separate workflow logic from raw model outputs, reducing prompt brittleness while supporting review and approval [1] [2]. More broadly, formal specifications and DSL frameworks are presented as enablers of verification, executable semantics, and trusted execution infrastructure in evolving language/tooling ecosystems [3] [4] [48].

The strongest direct relevance to SSEVOH-DSL is the idea that a self-modification should be treated as an explicit artifact rather than an implicit internal change. Inferred: this artifact-centric view is a prerequisite for attaching approval policies, preconditions, invariants, or rollback semantics to a modification. **Confidence level: Low** - this is a synthesis across specification and governance papers rather than a direct claim from a single source [1] [5].

### 3.2 Specific formalisms in the literature

**SpecMAS** provides perhaps the clearest specification-to-validation pipeline in the retrieved material: it begins with a natural-language SOP, extracts variables, transitions, and properties, compiles them into executable NuSMV code, and enforces validity through temporal-logic model checking; failed properties trigger an autonomous debugging loop until verification passes [11]. This is highly relevant to SSEVOH-DSL because it demonstrates how natural-language operational intent can be converted into a verifiable artifact before execution [11].

A **multi-agent schema language** is used in another system to specify agents and then execute them in a multi-agent prototype, but the excerpt does not expose a detailed validation or activation pipeline [13]. This illustrates a recurring gap: representation is often described more clearly than runtime governance [12] [13].

For planning-oriented workflows, natural-language instructions are translated into **PDDL and RDDL** specifications. Validation occurs through structured prompting, retrieval-augmented generation, and iterative refinement; repair mechanisms such as R3AG and R2EC either regenerate revised specifications or perform localized repair before final execution in training or RL settings [14]. This line of work shows that iterative repair can be embedded upstream of runtime activation, though it does not by itself constitute a trusted approval service [14].

**SystemPAD** encodes prescriptive knowledge as executable system knowledge interpreted at runtime by an interpreter agent, enabling interactive execution of requirements and policies rather than static reading [15]. This is relevant because it narrows the gap between written policy and runtime behavior [15].

The **ODP enterprise-language** work proposes DSL-based tooling for evolving requirements and deontic obligations, emphasizing lifecycle consistency and tool-chain support, but the surfaced evidence does not specify a concrete activation mechanism [12].

### 3.3 Synthesis: what formal languages solve and what they do not

Taken together, these papers show that formal or semi-formal representations can support:
- explicit change description [13] [15];  
- validation via model checking or structured repair loops [11] [14];  
- executable semantics that connect requirements to runtime behavior [15].  

But they also leave a consistent gap: many systems stop short of specifying how each approved change is safely activated, monitored, and potentially revoked in production [12] [13]. This gap directly motivates an SSEVOH-DSL architecture that couples formal change description with mandatory approval-mediated activation [5] [7].

**Confidence level: High** - the representation/activation gap is explicitly supported by the evidence synthesis [12] [13].

## 4. Trusted Approval, Governance, and Safe Promotion of Agent Updates

### 4.1 Trusted execution and verification infrastructure

The literature contains several activation-control patterns relevant to trusted mediation. **ENTRUST** uses a verified controller platform in which a trusted virtual machine interprets and executes the MAPE loop, with probabilistic model checking for assurance [16]. This is strong evidence that trusted execution infrastructure can be placed between adaptive logic and live deployment [16].

Related logic-based trust and ethics work argues that prior certification alone is insufficient for agents that modify beliefs, and therefore proposes **dynamic self-checking at runtime** [17]. This directly supports the claim that previously valid modifications may require continued re-evaluation [5] [17].

### 4.2 Human approval and policy gates

Human approval gates appear repeatedly in operational settings. In SOC automation, semi-autonomous modes require explicit human approval for critical or ambiguous cases, and even more autonomous levels reserve analyst review for critical actions [18]. HITL checkpoints are also recommended in agentic pipelines so operators can intervene before full workflow execution, including model or data-layer selection [22].

Autonomous-driving governance includes **batch pre-approval** at startup or after software updates, followed by reporting to a recorder for audit [20]. Although this is not a direct LLM-agent self-modification system, it provides a clear architectural pattern: approval before activation, then auditable runtime reporting [20].

### 4.3 Auditability, signed workflows, canary release, and rollback

Workflow integrity is mediated in one robot-execution architecture by **signed workflow queues**, which must be retrieved before the robot executes them [19]. Governance-first orchestration more generally relies on deterministic control planes, schema validation, policy engines, access controls, and tamper-evident or append-only logs [43] [44] [45].

Canary-style promotion is explicitly mentioned in predictive-maintenance architecture, where updates are released in a test environment before production [21]. Runtime adaptation frameworks also emphasize monitored feedback loops, drift alerting, rollback, sign-off, and properties such as determinism, completeness, and termination [6] [7] [8].

### 4.4 Comparison with informal prompt editing

The retrieved papers do not directly present a mature literature on informal prompt-editing workflows as governance systems; instead, the contrast emerges indirectly [46] [47] [60] [61] [62]. Compared with structured DSL and control-plane approaches, informal prompt editing lacks explicit schemas, validation artifacts, activation controls, rollback plans, and audit trails [1] [43] [44]. Therefore, a DSL-plus-trusted-service approach is best understood as shifting self-modification from an opaque model behavior into a governed operational process [2] [45].

**Confidence level: High** - the architecture-level governance patterns are strongly evidenced, though often from adjacent domains [16] [19] [20].

## 5. Empirical Performance of Self-Improving Agents

### 5.1 Failure-driven self-improvement

Self-improving agents use failures as inference-time or iterative signals in several ways: self-correction, self-generated feedback, reflection, and diagnosis before the next attempt [23] [24] [28] [63] [64]. Across several systems, these methods improve outcomes without additional training [49] [50] [51] [52].

TR-Agent is especially informative because it exposes both the promise and instability of self-improvement. After early volatility, many trials recover and exceed targets on IDM, MOBIL, and LWR tasks, with some LWR trials exceeding 90% improvement [25]. But the same study reports severe early regressions, including drops below -80% on IDM and large swings on MOBIL, showing that failure-guided search can be highly unstable before convergence [25].

A self-taught evaluator improves RewardBench accuracy from 75.4% to 88.3%, and to 88.7% with majority voting, through iterative synthetic preference data [27]. This is strong evidence that self-improvement can be materially effective under the right loop design [27].

### 5.2 Instability, miscalibration, and oversight needs

Performance gains do not eliminate the need for governance. In an exam-question generation study, one agent overestimated its own quality by roughly 1 to 1.5 points relative to a meta-evaluator, showing that self-reflection signals can themselves be biased [26]. More generally, adaptive systems perform best when transparent, overridable, and supported by feedback loops; otherwise, poorly timed or opaque adaptation can reduce trust and contribute to drift-like failure modes such as specification drift or cognitive tunneling [9] [10] [52] [59].

For SSEVOH-DSL, the practical implication is clear: self-improvement evidence supports allowing agents to propose modifications, but not to activate them autonomously without repeated control and re-validation [5] [25] [26].

**Confidence level: High** - the gain/instability duality is one of the clearest empirical patterns in the supplied evidence [25] [27].

## 6. Failure Modes: Tools, Retrieval, Prompt Injection, and Memory

### 6.1 Tool-use and retrieval failures

The strongest operational safety evidence concerns tools and retrieval. Retrieval-enabled systems can show **safety degradation**: broader external access may lower refusal rates, increase bias sensitivity, and increase harmful-content generation even when retrieval quality is high [35]. This shows that utility-enhancing changes can silently weaken safety properties [35].

Tool-use failures include incorrect tool selection, format violations, hallucinated API calls, parameter misassignment, and weak correction after error [31] [34]. One study reports that more than 90% of tool errors cannot be corrected under static imitation learning, underscoring the need for stronger runtime control and recovery [34].

Silent tool failures matter as much as obvious ones: one framework targets recovery from silent errors in both calculator and embodied-planning settings, while ChaosLLM highlights realistic non-adversarial faults such as crashes and timeouts [30] [32].

### 6.2 Prompt injection and unsafe external action

Prompt injection is a major agent failure mode. Direct and indirect injections can override system instructions, exfiltrate secrets, and trigger unauthorized actions [29]. OS-HARM similarly finds that computer-use agents are vulnerable to static prompt injections and can perform unsafe actions [33]. These findings strongly support approval-mediated gating for any self-modification that changes prompts, tool interfaces, or execution policies [29] [33].

### 6.3 Memory as an underdeveloped governance frontier

By contrast, the evidence on memory is much thinner. The supplied studies indicate that users often hold incomplete mental models of agent memory and want memory organized by category and task, but they do not provide strong empirical benchmarks for **memory drift** specifically [36]. This is a major gap for SSEVOH-DSL, because memory changes may create delayed or hard-to-audit harms even when prompt and tool updates are well governed [36].

Taken together, these findings suggest that tools and retrieval are the best-developed empirical targets for governance today, while memory governance remains a high-value research opportunity. **Confidence level: High** for the tool/retrieval bottleneck; **Low** for detailed memory-governance conclusions due to sparse direct evidence [35] [36].

## 7. Evaluation Beyond Success Rate

A major methodological trend in the supplied literature is the rejection of success-rate-only evaluation for autonomous agents [57] [58] [59]. **AGEE** explicitly argues that identical final answers can conceal meaningfully different trajectories and proposes multi-dimensional process-level metrics [40]. Another framework argues that evaluation should cover the full execution trajectory so that planning, tool use, memory, policies, and verification can be assessed separately and jointly [42].

The agentic-AI benchmark survey proposes a taxonomy and metric suite spanning process, governance, robustness, and efficiency because current benchmarks lack a unified lifecycle view [38]. **BEDI** similarly argues that task-level success obscures which sub-capability actually failed and therefore proposes capability-centered evaluation [37]. Planning-focused work adds execution-oriented metrics such as executability and average execution steps [39], while navigation and prediction benchmarks use path-level measures such as navigation error, SPL, nDTW, path efficiency, collision events, and trajectory acceptability or diversity [65] [66] [67] [68] [69].

For SSEVOH-DSL, this implies that evaluation should include more than downstream task accuracy. A governance-aware evaluation should at minimum measure:
- modification approval accuracy and latency [18] [20];  
- regression frequency after approved changes [25];  
- rollback frequency and revocation triggers [7] [8];  
- trajectory-level safety and tool correctness [31] [40];  
- persistence of gains over time under changing conditions [10] [42].  

The first three items are synthesis rather than directly named benchmark variables. **Confidence level: Medium** - strongly aligned with the literature’s evaluation logic, though not always listed in exactly these terms [38] [42].

## 8. Implications for SSEVOH-DSL

### 8.1 What the literature already justifies

The literature already justifies five core design commitments for SSEVOH-DSL:

1. **Represent changes explicitly** rather than letting them remain implicit prompt mutations [1] [2].  
2. **Validate before activation** using formal checks, repair loops, policy constraints, or human approval where appropriate [11] [14] [18].  
3. **Separate approval from execution** through a trusted service or control plane [16] [19] [43].  
4. **Monitor and revoke after deployment** because prior approval may become invalid over time [5] [7] [8] [17].  
5. **Evaluate lifecycle behavior, not only outcomes** [40] [42].

### 8.2 What could make SSEVOH-DSL distinct

The evidence does not show an existing framework that fully combines typed self-modification across prompts, tools, memory, and workflows with mandatory trusted pre-activation approval for every change [13] [20]. That creates a plausible novelty space. The most promising positioning is to present SSEVOH-DSL as a **control-plane language for agent evolution**, where each change unit carries:
- target surface;  
- expected benefit;  
- risk class;  
- supporting test evidence;  
- required approver;  
- canary scope;  
- rollback plan;  
- automatic revocation triggers [5] [7] [8] [45].  

This design is a synthesis from the governance literature rather than a directly retrieved prior system. **Confidence level: Medium** - well supported conceptually, but still an inferred design pattern [43] [45].

## 9. Research Gaps

The supplied evidence highlights several gaps directly relevant to future work:

- There is a lack of end-to-end systems that formally represent self-modifications and require trusted approval before each activation [13] [20].  
- Memory-update governance, drift detection, and rollback remain underdeveloped [36].  
- Comparative evidence between DSL-based governance and informal prompt-editing approaches is limited [46] [47].  
- Few benchmarks test whether a previously safe modification later becomes unsafe because tasks, environments, or tools changed [6] [10].  
- Integration between formal verification artifacts and real runtime enforcement, audit, and revocation remains incomplete [12] [43] [45].  

**Confidence level: High** - these gaps follow directly from repeated omissions and limitations in the retrieved evidence [12] [36].

## 10. Conclusion

The literature converges on a clear conclusion: self-evolving agents can improve substantially through reflection, repair, and adaptive revision, but those same mechanisms introduce regressions, hidden failures, and safety risks that make indefinite trust untenable [9] [10] [25] [27]. Formal specifications and DSLs improve auditability and validation, yet many existing approaches do not fully solve runtime approval and activation [11] [12] [13] [15]. In parallel, governance-first architectures consistently rely on trusted control planes, approval gates, signed workflows, canary promotion, monitoring, rollback, and tamper-evident logging to prevent unsafe or untraceable change activation [16] [19] [20] [21] [43] [44] [45].

Taken together, these findings provide strong support for SSEVOH-DSL’s core thesis: self-modifications should be explicit, typed, repeatedly validated, and approved by a trusted service before taking effect [5] [7] [8]. The largest open opportunities are end-to-end approval-mediated agent evolution, stronger memory governance, and lifecycle-aware benchmarks that test whether trust in approved changes remains valid over time [36] [38] [42].

**Overall confidence level: High** - the report’s central conclusions are supported by consistent cross-source convergence across formal-governance, trusted-activation, empirical self-improvement, and evaluation literatures [1] [5] [16] [25] [40].
